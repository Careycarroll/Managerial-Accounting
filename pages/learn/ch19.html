Process Costing Chapter 19

Spoilage, Rework, and Scrap

Distinguish normal from abnormal spoilage, determine how inspection point affects cost assignment, apply process costing with spoilage under weighted-average and FIFO methods, and account for rework and scrap under job and process costing.

Learning Objectives

  1. Distinguish normal spoilage from abnormal spoilage
  2. Identify the inspection point and explain how it determines which units bear spoilage costs
  3. Account for spoilage in process costing using the weighted-average method
  4. Account for spoilage in process costing using the FIFO method
  5. Account for rework in job costing and process costing
  6. Account for scrap using the production method and the sales method
Tool 1 of 5

Spoilage, Rework, and Scrap Classifier

Before diving into calculations, build conceptual clarity. Normal spoilage is inherent in an efficient production process and is absorbed into good unit costs. Abnormal spoilage exceeds normal levels and is expensed immediately. Rework fixes defective units. Scrap is a residual with low sales value. Classify each scenario.

The inspection point determines which units bear spoilage costs. Units that have not yet reached the inspection point when the period ends do not bear spoilage costs. Tool 3 of 5

Weighted-Average Process Costing with Spoilage

Extend the five-step process costing procedure to include normal and abnormal spoilage under the weighted-average method. Normal spoilage costs are spread across good completed units. Abnormal spoilage costs are expensed separately and never assigned to good units.

Tool 4 of 5

FIFO Process Costing with Spoilage

Under FIFO, beginning WIP costs are kept separate from current period costs. Spoilage is identified at the inspection point and classified as normal or abnormal. Normal spoilage is absorbed only by units that passed the inspection point this period.
